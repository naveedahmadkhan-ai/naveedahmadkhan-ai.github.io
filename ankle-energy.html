Project 02 · Ankle rehabilitation · 2026

Energy shaping with an attention-augmented neural operator for safe torque control of an ankle rehabilitation robot

In therapy, how the robot pushes the foot matters as much as where it moves it. Our controller builds an energy landscape around the target posture of the ankle. The pull back towards the target comes from the slope of that landscape, and added damping keeps the contact with the person safe. An attention-augmented Fourier neural operator (AFNO) reads the robot's sensors and keeps re-tuning the landscape for each person.

Published 2026 · Advanced Engineering Informatics · Open access ↗
Robot
Three-degree-of-freedom parallel ankle robot: four pneumatic muscles move the footplate through cables. Load cells, linear potentiometers and pressure sensors, with real-time control on dSPACE.
Methods
Port-Hamiltonian model · energy shaping (IDA-PBC) · Fourier neural operator with attention · physical human–robot interaction

01 · THE IDEA

Shape the energy, and the torque follows

Think of the target posture as the bottom of a bowl. The steeper the bowl, the harder the robot pulls the foot back, and damping takes energy out so the foot settles instead of swinging. No single bowl suits every person, so the AFNO keeps adjusting how steep it is and how much damping it adds. The target itself does not move.

02 · THE APPROACH

Learn the landscape, control with energy

The AFNO was trained on interaction data from ten volunteers. On the robot, it turns a short window of sensor data into 15 shaping parameters. The energy-shaping law converts them into joint torques, which the cables and pneumatic muscles then apply.

SENSE

What the person is doing

Joint angles and the torques between foot and footplate show whether the person is following, resisting or helping the movement.

LEARN

Patterns across the whole window

Fourier layers capture the shape of the motion over time; attention picks out the features that matter for that person and moment.

SHAPE

Torque from energy

The robot's torque comes from the slope of the shaped energy plus damping, which is designed to keep the interaction passive and bounded while the shape adapts.

TRACKING

Close to the planned motion

The average tracking error was 13.2%, 18.8% and 12.4% of the movement range for inversion/eversion, dorsiflexion/plantarflexion and abduction/adduction. The largest deviations stayed below 27.6%, 33.3% and 22.4%.

Errors like these can briefly under- or over-assist at abrupt changes, so the paper proposes using the controller with conservative range, torque and speed limits and a supervisor that watches the error.

ENERGY

Less energy spent on corrections

With the AFNO, the energy-efficiency gain was 20.0% (FNO 16.0%, Transformer 14.1%, LSTM 11.2%) and the residual energy was the lowest, at 0.491. The largest torque deviation also stayed lowest on every axis, at 12.3–12.7 Nm, well within the robot's torque capacity.

05 · SCOPE

What this shows, and what comes next

A feasibility study with healthy volunteers, approved by the University of Canberra Human Research Ethics Committee (No. 202514641).

PARTICIPANTS

Ten healthy volunteers

Eight men and two women aged 25–43, some imitating constrained, stroke-like movement patterns. No patients took part yet.

SAFETY

Passive by construction

Because the torque comes from a shaped energy with added damping, the interaction stays within bounded, passive limits. A full proof for parameters that change over time is future work.

NEXT

Towards patients

Testing with stroke survivors, adding muscle-activity (EMG) feedback, and reference motions taken from clinical populations.

DEPLOYMENT

Built for real time

The model is deployed with TensorRT at FP16 precision, with a target of under 10 ms per prediction.
