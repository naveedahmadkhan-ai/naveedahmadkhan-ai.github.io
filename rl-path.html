Project 03 · Ankle rehabilitation · 2025

Reinforcement learning that plans energy-efficient ankle exercises for stroke survivors

After a stroke, the ankle is moved by weak and unbalanced muscles, so a fixed exercise path can overload some muscles and waste effort. We combined a musculoskeletal model of the ankle, the energy exchanged between patient and robot, and reinforcement learning. The learning agent adjusts the exercise path step by step. It keeps the path close to the planned motion while lowering joint torque, muscle strain and the work the patient has to do.

Published 2025 · IEEE Trans. Neural Syst. Rehabil. Eng. · Open access ↗
Robot
Three-degree-of-freedom parallel ankle robot: four pneumatic muscles move the footplate through cables, with load cells, linear potentiometers and pressure sensors.
Methods
Hill-type musculoskeletal model · EMG features · transactive energy · reinforcement learning with proximal policy optimisation (PPO)
Team

01 · THE PROBLEM

Paths that ignore the muscles

Most path planners aim only at the joint angles. For a stroke survivor that can mean jerky motion, overworked calf muscles and wasted effort: studies estimate that 30–50% of the energy spent in conventional rehabilitation is lost to inefficient motion and interaction. Our agent is rewarded for the whole picture.

02 · THE APPROACH

Model the muscles, track the energy, learn the path

Muscle activity and joint motion feed a musculoskeletal model. The energy exchanged between patient and robot is added on top, and a PPO agent learns which small changes to the path give the best reward.

MODEL

What the muscles do

EMG features and a Hill-type model estimate each muscle's force and the moments it creates at the ankle.

ENERGY

Where the energy goes

Kinetic and potential energies of patient and robot show when motion wastes effort or loads the joint.

LEARN

A better path, step by step

The PPO agent makes small path changes and keeps those that make motion accurate, smooth and easy on the muscles.

04 · RESULTS

Measured with ten stroke patients

MUSCLES

Calf muscles do most of the work

The gastrocnemius muscles peaked at 950–1100 N, 60–75% of the total muscle force. The tibialis anterior, which lifts the foot, produced only 50–210 N, and the peroneus 120–370 N. Stroke patients lean on their plantarflexors.

TRAINING

A more balanced, smoother movement

Over training, the agent cut plantarflexor dominance by almost 15% and raised dorsiflexor engagement by 25%. Ab/Ad corrections improved by about 71%, and torque variability fell by 30%.

05 · SCOPE

Study and next steps

Approved by the Institutional Research and Ethics Committee of Nazarbayev University (No. 683/27022023). All participants gave informed consent.

PARTICIPANTS

Ten stroke patients

Mildly impaired, eight men and two women aged 28–52, 4–6 months after their stroke.

TWO MODES

Free and guided motion

In zero-impedance mode the patient moved freely; in trajectory-tracking mode the robot guided the passive foot along the path.

MEASURED

Muscles and motion

EMG of four ankle muscles at 2 kHz, filtered at 20–450 Hz, with interaction forces, torques, joint positions and velocities.

NEXT

Personalised and real time

Personalised learning models, real-time adaptive control and long-term tracking of each patient's progress.
