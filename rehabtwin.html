Project 01 · Upper-limb rehabilitation · 2026

A calibrated digital twin that teaches a DeepONet to control a rehabilitation robot

A rehabilitation robot has to follow the planned exercise closely, but its joints lag behind the command, sag under gravity and stick each time the motion reverses. I built a digital twin of our upper-limb robot, calibrated it on the real hardware, and used it to teach a DeepONet how to correct any exercise before it starts. The robot needs no repetitions with the patient attached and no retraining when the therapist plans a new exercise.

Paper under review · IEEE/ASME Transactions on Mechatronics

Upper-limb exoskeleton built in our lab: 3D-printed links, four maxon motors with EPOS4 drives (three shoulder joints, one elbow)

01 · THE PROBLEM

Why the robot misses the plan

The drive's PID loop only reacts after an error appears. So the joint trails the command, gravity pulls it below the plan, and friction holds it still for a moment at every reversal. A correction computed in advance can cancel most of this.

02 · THE APPROACH

Learn in the twin, correct on the robot

Swipe sideways to see the whole diagram →

CALIBRATE

A twin of the whole chain

The twin models the drive's own controller as well as the mechanics, so it reproduces the lag, the gravity sag and the stick plateaus seen on the real joint.

LEARN

Correction as an operator

The DeepONet takes the whole planned exercise and returns a correction for every time step. A new exercise needs neither repetitions nor retraining.

CORRECT

Bounded and checked in advance

The correction is limited and computed before the joint moves, so it can be checked before anything reaches the robot, whatever the network outputs.

TWIN QUALITY MATTERS

A network trained on the CAD model is not enough

The same DeepONet trained in an uncalibrated CAD twin learned the wrong gravity and no friction. With a freshly calibrated twin, the calibrated network was 50% better on the gravity-loaded joint and had the lowest error on every joint tested.

After we removed the forearm module, the twin was out of date and the CAD-trained network won on one joint. The measured twin–real mismatch predicted this, as our error bound says, and a few minutes of re-calibration per joint fixes it.

WITH PEOPLE ATTACHED

Two volunteers, no stop needed

With a volunteer's arm in the robot, the correction lowered the error by 65–80% and the worst-case error from about 1° to 0.23–0.45°, with the same effort. The second volunteer loaded the main joint a third more, and the error did not change.

05 · GUARANTEES

Why it is safe to use

Every condition below was evaluated with the parameters measured on our robot, not with assumed values.

STABILITY

The inner loop stays stable

Practical input-to-state stability of the drive's feedback loop, including stick–slip friction and sensor quantisation.

SAFETY ENVELOPE

Holds for any network output

Because the correction is bounded, the joint stays inside a known envelope even if the network is wrong. The largest correction ever applied was 1.4°, against a bound of 4°.

SIM-TO-REAL BOUND

Where the real error comes from

The error on the robot is bounded by three parts: the best the twin allows, the network's own error, and the twin–real mismatch, which can be measured during routine use.

TRAINING LABELS

Iterative learning converges

The corrections computed in the twin converge to a small neighbourhood of the optimum, so the network learns from consistent labels.
